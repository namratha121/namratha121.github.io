Academic project · Customer Analytics

Creative Gaming Uplift Modeling

Finding the players an in-app ad actually persuades to buy, instead of the ones who would have bought anyway, to get the most profit from an ad campaign.

Course
Customer Analytics, UC San Diego
Tools
Python, Polars, pyrsm, scikit-learn, XGBoost, plotnine
Team
Namratha K, Aanya Sikka, Yug Gadhiya
View code on GitHub ↗

The problem

Creative Gaming's mobile game Space Pirates launched a $14.99 in-app campaign, and adoption was slower than expected. The team tested an in-app ad to drive purchases. The question was not "who is likely to buy?" but "who buys because of the ad?", since showing an ad to someone who would buy anyway adds cost without adding sales.

Data

  • A randomized experiment: 30,000 players saw the ad and 30,000 did not.
  • The ad roughly doubled conversion, from 6% without it to 13% with it.
  • Player behavior such as game level, playtime, sessions and past purchases, split 70/30 into training and test sets.

How we approached it

PROPENSITY MODEL
  • Predicts how likely a player is to buy if shown the ad.
  • Tends to target "sure things" who would buy anyway.
UPLIFT MODEL
  • Trains one model on players who saw the ad and one on players who didn't.
  • Uplift = chance of buying with the ad minus chance without it.
FOUR ALGORITHMS
  • Built both approaches with logistic regression, a neural network, a random forest and XGBoost.
  • Tuned each with cross-validated grid search.
PROFIT TEST
  • Scaled to 120,000 players and targeted the top 25% (30,000) under each model.
  • Incremental profit = extra buyers × $14.99 − $1.50 per player targeted.

Results

Incremental profit from targeting 30,000 players:

AlgorithmUplift modelPropensity modelUplift advantage
Random forest$58,066$47,382+$10,684
Neural network$56,997$48,719+$8,277
XGBoost$56,373$43,046+$13,327
Logistic regression$45,908––
  • Every uplift model beat its propensity counterpart. The best, a random forest, earned $58,066, about 23% more than targeting the same players by likelihood to buy.
  • For roughly the bottom 10% of players, the ad lowered the chance of buying. Uplift modeling identifies these players so the campaign can leave them alone.
  • Uplift models favored a smaller target (about 25–35% of players) than propensity models (45–50%), so the campaign spends less and earns more.

Impact

By targeting only the 25% of players most persuaded by the ad, the uplift model earns $58K in incremental profit, 23% more than propensity targeting and $70K better than random targeting, which loses money.
